# Slitherlink

> A field of numbers hiding a single, silent loop — count the sides, not the cells.

**At a glance** · Solo · one puzzle, one answer · 10–20 min · Play it under **Logic** (`/logic`).

## What it is

Slitherlink (also called Fences or Loop the Loop) is a loop-drawing puzzle on a grid of dots. Some cells carry a number; that number tells you exactly how many of the cell's four edges belong to the loop. There is never any ambiguity about the final shape — a well-formed Slitherlink has **exactly one** loop that satisfies every clue, and it is always reachable by pure deduction, never by trial and error. What makes it distinct from most shading puzzles is that the object you're solving for is a *path*, not a region: every deduction ultimately comes down to how many lines can meet at a single dot, and a loop only ever passes through a dot cleanly or not at all.

## Goal

Draw a single closed loop along the grid lines so that **every numbered cell has precisely that many of its four sides used by the loop**, and the loop never branches, crosses itself, or splits into more than one piece.

## The grid & clues

The board is a 6×6 grid of cells (a 7×7 lattice of dots), which is the fixed size used throughout ChessRetabled — there's no separate difficulty selector, but every puzzle is freshly generated so no two sessions repeat. Each cell shows either a clue **0–3** or is left blank (no constraint). Internally the puzzle is generated by growing a random "inside" region of cells and reading the boundary of that region as the loop — so every generated board is guaranteed solvable, and clues are stripped out one at a time only while the remaining set still pins down a unique solution.

## Rules

1. Draw line segments along the dot-grid so they form **exactly one closed loop**.
2. A numbered cell must have **exactly that many** of its four edges on the loop (0, 1, 2, or 3). A blank-adjacent value of 4 never occurs — every side used is impossible on a single cell only if it would force self-crossing, so clues only ever run 0–3.
3. Blank cells have no constraint — the loop may use any number of their sides.
4. The loop may not cross itself or touch itself at a dot: every dot the loop visits has **exactly two** loop edges touching it, never one, three, or four.
5. The loop must be a single connected cycle — no separate disconnected segments, and no dead-end spurs.

## How it works in ChessRetabled

- **Opening a puzzle.** Under **Logic**, choose Slitherlink. Every board is a fresh 6×6 lattice, generated by growing a random connected "inside" region and reading off the boundary as the true loop; clues are then thinned to the minimum that still forces a unique solution, so no two boards feel identical even though the size never changes.
- **Drawing edges.** Each edge on the lattice is a button you click to cycle through three states: **blank → line → ✕ (ruled out) → blank**. Marking an edge ✕ isn't required to win, but it's a scratchpad for edges you've proven *can't* be part of the loop — very useful once you start cross-hatching between neighbouring clues.
- **The 💡 hint.** Every generated puzzle stores its full solution edge set, so a hint never has to re-solve anything. Press **Hint** for a *nudge* — one missing loop edge is highlighted; press again to *reveal*, drawing that edge in for you. Using a hint marks the puzzle **assisted** in your history and Dashboard, keeping unaided solves distinct.
- **Completion.** The board is solved the moment your drawn edges form one closed loop that satisfies every clue; it's logged to your archive immediately, no separate "submit" step needed.

## Strategy

### Beginner — the two extremes
- **Zero cells never touch the loop.** All four sides of a 0 cell are guaranteed blank — mark them with ✕ so you don't accidentally draw one later while working on a neighbouring clue.
- **Three cells are almost fully drawn.** A 3 cell has exactly one blank side; you often can't yet tell which, but any side it *shares* with another clue you've already resolved is easy to fill in, narrowing the remaining ambiguity to a single edge.
- **Corner and edge cells have fewer sides.** A corner cell only has two possible edges (both on the border); a "2" corner clue forces both of them onto the loop immediately, a "1" corner clue is genuinely ambiguous but a "0" corner clue eliminates both border edges outright.
- **Two adjacent clues that differ by exactly the right amount often share a forced edge.** If a 3 sits directly next to a 0, the edge between them must be blank (from the 0) — which changes the 3's other three sides from "3 of 4" to "3 of the remaining 3," forcing every one of them.
- **Start from the densest cluster of clues.** A tight group of 2s and 3s constrains each other far more than an isolated clue surrounded by blanks — work there first to build a foothold.

### Intermediate — pairing and diagonal tricks
- **Diagonal 3-3.** Two 3-clues touching only at a corner force both of the far outer edges (away from the shared corner) onto the loop, because each 3 can spare only one blank side and the shared corner can't be the reason for both simultaneously.
- **Diagonal 1-1 or 0 near a 1.** Adjacent low clues often force a corner's two edges to both be blank, or exactly one-and-only-one to be drawn — track the dot's total degree (it must end at 0 or 2, never 1 or 3) to eliminate options quickly.
- **Every dot has degree 0 or 2.** This is the loop's most powerful local rule: once a dot already has one line touching it, every other edge at that dot is *forced* — either a second line completes the pass-through, or (once the dot's degree is already satisfied at 2) all remaining edges at that dot become ✕.
- **Follow the ✕ marks across shared edges.** An edge you've ruled out at one clue often removes an option at the clue on the other side of that same edge — always re-check both cells that share an edge you just resolved before moving on.
- **Use a "3 next to a 3" straight pair.** Two 3-clues sharing a full edge (not just a corner) force that shared edge itself to be a line in almost every configuration, since leaving it blank would require both cells to draw all three of their remaining sides in a way that usually creates a stray branch.

### Advanced — global consistency
- **The loop can't touch itself.** Even if two separate loop segments would each individually satisfy their local clues, if they'd have to cross or touch at a shared dot, that configuration is illegal — use this to rule out an otherwise-plausible local solution once your partial loop starts to wrap back near itself.
- **Region parity.** Because the loop is literally the boundary between an "inside" and "outside" region of cells, two cells separated by a known-blank edge are the *same* region (both inside or both outside), while two cells separated by a known-line edge are in *different* regions. Chain this "same/different region" logic across the whole board like a two-colouring problem — it often forces cells far from any clue.
- **Work backward from a near-complete corner.** When one corner of the board is almost entirely resolved, propagate the forced dot-degree and region-parity rules outward; Slitherlink solutions tend to complete in a cascade once roughly two-thirds of the edges are fixed.
- **Guess-and-check as a last resort, but verify with parity, not luck.** If you're truly stuck, tentatively assume one ambiguous edge is a line, propagate the consequences via dot-degree and region-parity, and see if you reach a contradiction (a dot forced to degree 3, or two separate closed loops) — that's a legitimate deductive technique, not a guess, because a contradiction proves the opposite choice.

## Traps & common mistakes

- **Forgetting the dot-degree rule.** A common error is drawing three edges into one dot, which is never legal — the loop passes straight through, it doesn't branch.
- **Leaving a clue "locally satisfied" but globally wrong.** A configuration can satisfy every number and still form two separate small loops instead of one big one — always sanity-check the loop is a single piece near the end, not several disconnected rings.
- **Not marking ✕.** Skipping the "ruled out" markings makes it easy to accidentally draw an edge you already proved was impossible, undoing earlier deductions without noticing.
- **Ignoring blank cells.** Blank cells still participate in dot-degree logic even though they carry no number themselves — don't treat them as irrelevant just because they show no clue.
- **Chasing one clue in isolation for too long.** Slitherlink solves fastest when you bounce between clues that share edges; fixating on a single stubborn cell often means the missing deduction lives one cell over.

## A worked example

A 3 cell diagonally touching a 0 cell:

```
 . . . .
 . 0 3 .      The 0's four sides are all blank (✕).
 . . . .      The shared corner dot between 0 and 3
 . . . .      therefore can't get a line from the 0's
              side — so the 3's two edges touching
              that same corner must both be lines,
              since the 3 needs 3 of 4 sides drawn
              and only 3 non-zero-adjacent sides remain.
```

Two of the 3's edges are now forced without ever considering the whole board — a good reminder that Slitherlink deductions almost always start from the most extreme clues (0 and 3) and radiate outward.

## Glossary

- **Loop edge** — a grid segment that is part of the final loop.
- **Dot degree** — the number of loop edges touching a lattice point; always 0 or 2.
- **Inside/outside region** — the two areas the loop separates; a cell's region flips exactly when you cross a loop edge.
- **Cross-hatching** — marking edges ✕ once you've proven they can't be on the loop, to keep candidates visible.
